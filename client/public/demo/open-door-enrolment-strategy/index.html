The campaign ran for two weeks. The decision takes six.

Enrolment marketing started a fortnight before term and stopped on the first day of it. That timing came from the budget cycle, not from any observation of how a family actually decides.

Because the decision is not one decision. It is a conversation with a partner, a look at what it costs against what else that money was for, a rearrangement of who does the school run on a Wednesday, and — usually last — a comparison with the two other places nearby.

Nothing about the messages was wrong. They simply arrived after most of that had already happened, at the only point where the honest answer was "not this term".

What the fortnight missed

Week −6One parent first wonders about it. Tells nobody.
Week −5Mentioned at home. First objection raised: cost.
Week −4Quiet look at three options. No contact with any of them.
Week −3The childcare problem is identified. This is where most drop out.
Week −2The old campaign starts here.
Week −1Decision made or deferred to next term.

Six weeks, staged to the question being asked

The bar under each week is relative message volume. It is deliberately low at the start — early contact that pushes is what makes a family stop reading before the point at which they were going to be interested. Select a week.

The decision is rarely made by one person

Almost every enrolment involves a second adult who never visits the website, never opens the email, and holds an effective veto. Most campaigns have nothing at all for that person, which means the parent who is interested has to argue the case unaided from memory.

What normally happens

The enthusiastic parent reads everything, gets it, and then has to summarise a forty-minute open evening across a kitchen table at nine at night, from memory, to someone who is tired and was not asked.

The objection that comes back is almost always cost or logistics — the two things a brochure written to inspire does not address at all.

What is provided instead

A single side of A4, deliberately unglamorous, written for the person who was not there.

One page, sent after any enquiry
  • What it costs, per term, with everything included listed and everything extra named
  • Exact days and times, and the latest pick-up that is actually possible
  • What happens if you miss a week
  • How to stop, and what notice is required
  • Two sentences on what the child would actually be doing

No photographs, no adjectives, no mission statement. It is designed to survive being read sideways on someone else's phone.

Deadline pressure, only in week six

There is a real deadline — places are finite and the register closes. Saying so in week one would be true and would still be a mistake: at that stage the family has not yet decided they want it, and urgency applied to an undecided person reads as a sales technique rather than as information.

By week six the position has changed. A family that has read the cost page, spoken to a member of staff and worked out the Wednesday pick-up is no longer being pressured by a closing date — they are being reminded of one that genuinely affects them.

So: no countdowns, no "only three places left" in weeks one to five, and one plain sentence in week six. If the deadline is real, it only needs saying once.

Concept strategy for an invented learning centre. There are no intake numbers, enquiry counts or conversion figures on this page, and the six-week decision period is a working assumption drawn from how the decision is described rather than a measured finding — on a live campaign the first task would be asking twenty existing parents when they first thought about it. The insight being offered is calendar alignment, not better wording.
