Table SeasonCampaign structure · concept · 2026

Discount the empty night. Never the full one.

Friday and Saturday sold out most weeks. Tuesday and Wednesday ran at under a third. The instinct in the room was a blanket twenty per cent off, and it would have worked — it would have filled midweek and quietly moved a proportion of the weekend bookings onto cheaper nights.

That is the trap. A discount visible to everyone gets used by the people who were already coming, on the nights that were already full.

So the midweek offer was built as a separate thing. Its own name, its own short menu, its own audience, and — deliberately — its own look, so that a regular Friday booker never sees it as the same restaurant made cheaper.

The audience is not the occasion diner who books three weeks ahead. It is the person who lives within a mile and decides at six o'clock that they cannot be bothered to cook.

The week, before and after

Covers as a share of capacity. The point of the comparison is not that midweek goes up — that is easy. It is that Friday and Saturday do not move, because nothing on offer gives a weekend booker a reason to shift.

Two offers that must never blur

The clearest way to protect the weekend is to make the midweek proposition genuinely different — fewer dishes, no bookings, an earlier kitchen, and a price that is lower because the offer is smaller, not because the restaurant is discounting itself.

Six weeks, timed to when the decision is made

A midweek dinner is not planned. It is decided somewhere between four and six in the afternoon, usually by someone tired, standing in a kitchen with nothing in the fridge. Every message in the launch is placed against that moment rather than against a marketing calendar.

The guardrails

Written down because the pressure to break them arrives around week three, on the first Tuesday that rains.

Nothing is ever discounted on Thursday, Friday or Saturday.

Not a set menu, not a percentage, not a "pre-theatre" version. The weekend is the profit and it is not available to be traded for volume.

Table Season is never described as cheaper.

It is smaller. Six dishes instead of eighteen, one service instead of two, no reservations desk. The price reflects the offer. The moment it is framed as a discount, the main room is devalued along with it.

No bookings, ever.

It is the operational reason the price works, and it is also the thing that keeps the occasion diner away from it. A booking system would quietly turn it into a cheap version of the weekend.

The two are marketed to different lists.

Weekend regulars are not emailed about midweek. Someone who only ever comes on a Tuesday is not sent the tasting menu announcement. Overlap is allowed to happen naturally and is never engineered.

If midweek fills, the price goes up before the covers do.

The failure mode of a successful midweek offer is that it stays cheap forever out of nervousness. Reviewed at week twelve.
