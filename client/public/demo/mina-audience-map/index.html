“Women, 25–45” is a demographic, not an audience.

A candle brand was writing every email, advert and product page for women 25–45. Three entirely different people were buying, for three incompatible reasons, and all three were receiving the same argument.

Splitting them by age would not have helped — the gift buyer and the self-treat buyer are frequently the same age, sometimes the same person in different months. The useful line is why they are buying.

Segment

The gift buyer

Buying for someone else, usually under time pressure

The self-treat buyer

Buying for themselves, usually as a small compensation

The wholesale buyer

Buying to resell, on a margin calculation
In their words

“It needs to look like I put thought into it and arrive before Saturday.”

“It has been a week. I am allowed one nice thing.”

“Will it sell at twenty-eight pounds, and can you restock in ten days?”

What they are really buying

Certainty. That it arrives on time, looks considered, and does not need wrapping.

Permission. A small, defensible purchase that is not extravagant.

Predictability. Consistent stock, consistent margin, no surprises for their own customers.

Message angle

Lead with the packaging and the delivery date. The candle is almost secondary — they are buying an act of thoughtfulness with a deadline attached.

Lead with the ritual, not the object. When it gets lit, what it replaces, why the thirty-five hours matter. Price appears late and calmly.

Lead with the numbers. Trade price, minimum order, lead time, restock window. No brand storytelling — they will write their own.

Primary objection

“What if it does not arrive in time?”
Answered with a cut-off date shown on the page, not in the FAQ.

“Twenty-eight pounds for a candle is silly.”
Answered with burn hours and cost per evening, without being defensive about it.

“Can you actually supply consistently?”
Answered with a published lead time and an honest out-of-stock policy.

What works
  • Delivery cut-off on every product page
  • Gift note at checkout, free
  • Bundles that remove a decision
  • Search, in the two weeks before an occasion
  • Email, to people who already bought
  • Scent described in plain nouns
  • Refill, as a reason to come back
  • Slow, unhurried photography
  • A trade page that does not need an email to see prices
  • A downloadable line sheet
  • Direct outreach to twelve named shops
  • Replying within a working day
What to stop sending them

The founder's story email. They have four days and no interest.

Urgency and countdown timers. Pressure turns a small treat into a pushy transaction.

Everything consumer. They are on a different list and should never see a retail discount.

Where each one is worth the effort

Three segments and a small team means the channel plan has to say no somewhere. The blocks are relative priority, not a budget split — and two channels are marked as deliberate refusals.

ChannelGift buyerSelf-treatWholesale
Search — product and occasion terms
Email to existing customers
Organic social
Direct outreach to shops
Paid social — seasonal only
Influencer gifting — refused No mechanism to tell which of the three segments it reaches, and the brand cannot afford a channel it cannot read.
Marketplace listings — refused Competes on price with the wholesale accounts the brand is asking to stock it. The two cannot both be true.

Gift demand is not constant. Plan it that way.

The single biggest planning error would be to treat the three segments as equal all year. Two of them are steady. One is violently seasonal, and it is the one most likely to consume the whole budget in November if nobody has drawn this.

Relative demand shape, not measured data. Wholesale peaks in September and October because shops buy ahead of a season the consumer has not thought about yet — which means the trade line sheet has to be ready two months before the gift campaign starts.
